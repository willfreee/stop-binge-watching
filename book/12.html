The Easy Way to Stop Binge-Watching

12: The Illusion of Moderation

Have you ever noticed that the people who don't have a problem with alcohol never need to set rules about how much they drink?

They don't say, "Two glasses of wine with dinner, max. Only on weekends. I'll finish this bottle and then cut back." They don't need to. The thought never occurs to them. They drink when they genuinely want to, they stop when they've had enough, and the whole question of "rules" is irrelevant.

Now think about your own relationship with streaming. How many times have you made rules?

Two episodes a night. No more. Only on weekends. I'll finish this season and then I'll take a break. No bingeing anymore. This time I'll actually stick to it.

The rules sound reasonable. They feel like control—like the responsible, adult solution. But the very existence of the rules tells you something important, something the Big Monster doesn't want you to notice.

When you have to make rules about something, what does that tell you about your relationship to it?

It tells you that the thing has power over you. That you can't trust yourself with it. That left to your own devices, you'd consume it in a way you don't want to consume it. People who have a healthy relationship with something don't need to regulate it. The need for rules is the evidence of the problem.

You've made these rules before. We both know how it goes.

The first few days go well. You stick to your two-episode limit, pat yourself on the back, feel disciplined, in control, maybe even a little smug. See? I can handle this. I just needed some structure.

Then a hard day arrives. The kind of day that leaves you drained and hollow and desperate for relief. And the two-episode rule stretches. Just one more, you tell yourself. It's been a hard day. I deserve it. The rule bends.

Then a weekend arrives. Well, it's the weekend. The rules don't really apply on weekends. The rule stretches further.

Then the season finale hits, and you can't possibly stop now — not when you're so close to the end. I'll finish this season, and then I'll cut back. The rule, already threadbare, dissolves completely.

The rules were never the solution. They were the evidence of the problem. And the problem isn't that you lack discipline. The problem is that you still believe the queue contains something worth rationing.

That's the hidden premise underneath every attempt at moderation. It's the belief that the screen gives you something valuable — something precious enough that it must be portioned out carefully, like a rare delicacy.

Moderation seems like the best of both worlds—you keep the pleasure but lose the guilt, you get to keep the thing you enjoy while demonstrating that you're in control. It sounds like the smart solution.

But underneath that reasonable surface, something else is happening. The screen is still on a pedestal. The queue is still seen as a source of value. The Big Monster is still alive, still whispering that streaming gives you something worth protecting.

If someone told you they'd learned to moderate their drinking by only having two glasses of poison a night instead of six, would you congratulate them — or ask why they're still drinking poison?

The question sounds absurd. But it exposes the flaw in the moderation logic. If the thing you're consuming is genuinely harmful — if it's draining your energy, fogging your mind, stealing your sleep, and leaving you more exhausted than before — then the problem isn't the amount. The problem is the thing itself.

Moderation doesn't solve the problem. It just manages it. It keeps the poison flowing, just in smaller doses. And as long as you're still drinking it, you're still under its power.

The erosion of the rules isn't a failure of willpower. It's the logical result of a belief that never changed.

Think about what happened every time you tried to moderate. The rules held for a while. Then life got hard, or the weekend arrived, or the cliffhanger hit. And the rules broke. Not because you're weak. Not because you didn't try hard enough. But because underneath the rules, the core belief was still intact:

The screen gives me something valuable. It's a reward. It's a comfort. It's a source of relief.

As long as that belief persists, the rules will always erode. Because the rules are fighting the belief, and the belief is deeper. The rules say stop after two. The belief says this is precious — don't let it go. And when the two conflict, the belief wins. Every time.

If you believed a slot machine might pay out, how long would "just three pulls a night" last?

Not long. The moment the machine started flashing, the moment the near-miss hit, the moment the hope flared — the rule would vanish. Because the rule was never stronger than the belief. It was just a thin line drawn in sand, and the tide of belief washed it away.

Now you might be thinking, But without moderation, won't I just binge endlessly? Don't I need rules to keep myself in check?

That's the fear that keeps the moderation illusion alive. It's the belief that without rules, without limits, without self-imposed boundaries, you'd spiral into chaos. The screen would swallow you whole.

But think about the things you genuinely don't need to moderate. The things you enjoy but aren't compelled by. Maybe it's a walk in the park. Maybe it's a cup of tea in the morning. Maybe it's a book you pick up and put down freely. Do you need rules for those things? Do you need limits? Do you need to tell yourself, Only one walk a day. No more than two cups of tea. Don't read too much?

No. Because those things don't control you. You engage with them when you want to, and you stop when you're done. There's no friction. No negotiation. No rules.

Do you need rules to limit your consumption of the things that don't control you?

Of course not. The need for rules is the symptom of compulsion. When the compulsion is gone, the need for rules disappears with it.

Real freedom isn't managed consumption. It's not needing to manage. When the illusion collapses — when the queue is seen for what it actually is — there's nothing to moderate. The screen loses its value. The pedestal crumbles. The thing you were rationing reveals itself as nothing worth rationing.

You don't need rules to limit something you no longer crave. You just don't reach for it. Not because you're disciplined, but because there's nothing there worth reaching for.

Here's the reframe:

Moderation isn't freedom — it's rationed captivity. The moment you try to limit the screen, you've confirmed that the queue contains something worth rationing. And as long as that belief persists, the rules will erode. Real freedom isn't managing the trap. It's seeing that there's nothing valuable inside it to manage.

That's the shift this entire book has been building toward. Not learning to control the screen. Not finding the right rules. Not becoming disciplined enough to stick to the limits.

Seeing. Clearly, completely, irreversibly.

When you see that the queue was never a source of value — that it was a machine designed to extract your attention while giving back only the illusion of reward — the desire to moderate evaporates. You don't want two episodes. You don't want one. You don't want any, because you finally understand that there was never anything there you actually wanted.

Freedom isn't portion control. It's seeing that there's nothing to portion.

But the Big Monster has one more costume waiting. It's the comparison defense — the voice that says:

At least it's not social media. At least it's not something worse. This is harmless compared to other things.
