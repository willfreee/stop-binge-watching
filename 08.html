The Easy Way to Stop Binge-Watching

8: The Illusion of Cultural Relevance

When was the last time someone actually asked you, "Have you seen that show?" — and the conversation lasted more than ninety seconds?

Think about it honestly. Not the online discussions. Not the comment sections. Not the vague sense that everyone is talking about something. Actual, real-life, face-to-face conversations. Someone mentions a show. You say you've seen it or you haven't. There's a brief exchange — It's good. You should watch it. The ending is crazy. — and then the conversation moves on to something else.

Ninety seconds—maybe two minutes if the show is particularly popular.

Now compare that to the hours you've spent watching shows you weren't genuinely interested in, just so you could participate in those ninety-second exchanges. The math doesn't add up. But the fear that drives it feels very real.

Everyone is watching this. If I don't watch it, I'll have nothing to talk about. I'll be left out. I'll be the only one who doesn't get the references.

That fear has kept more queues open than almost any other. It's time to look at it clearly.

You know the moment. You're scrolling through the app, and you see it — the show everyone seems to be discussing. It's all over social media. Your coworkers mentioned it. Your friends posted about it. The cultural moment is happening, and you're not part of it.

A small pang of anxiety rises in your chest. I should watch this, you think. Just so I can participate. Just so I'm not the only one who doesn't know.

So you press play. Not because you're genuinely curious. Not because the story pulled you in. But because the thought of being outside the loop feels worse than the thought of losing several hours to something you don't actually care about.

Now let's examine that fear for a moment. When you picture being "in the loop," what do you actually picture? A deep, fulfilling conversation about the show's themes, its characters, its artistic choices? A meaningful exchange that leaves you feeling connected and understood?

Or do you picture something much simpler: the relief of not feeling left out?

For most people, it's the second thing. The actual conversations about shows are almost always brief. They're surface-level. They're the kind of exchange that fills a pause in a larger conversation and then evaporates. Have you seen it? Yeah. Is it good? It's decent. The acting is great. And then someone changes the subject.

The fear was never about the conversation. The conversation was never that deep. The fear was about the feeling of being outside — that vague, uncomfortable sense that something is happening and you're not part of it.

But here's the thing: That feeling isn't caused by the show. It's caused by the Parasite.

Remember the Parasite from Chapter 5 — the mechanism that makes you fear the absence of the screen, that whispers you'll be bored, you'll be empty, you'll be trapped if you stop watching? That same Parasite has a social variant. And it's very good at its job.

When you feel the dread of being the only person in the room who doesn't know what everyone's talking about — when you picture a group laughing at a reference and you standing there, smiling awkwardly, pretending to get it — that's not evidence of a real need. That's the Parasite adapting its message to a social context.

The Parasite doesn't just whisper you'll be bored without the screen. It whispers you'll be irrelevant without the screen. You'll be out of the loop. You'll be the one person who doesn't belong.

And that fear works. It works because humans are social creatures. We genuinely want to belong. We genuinely want to participate in shared culture. That's a real hunger — a valid, human need for connection and community.

But the Parasite hijacks that hunger and points it at the queue. The way to belong, it whispers, is to watch everything. The way to be relevant is to consume. The way to be part of the conversation is to never stop scrolling.

And we believe it. We believe it so thoroughly that we spend hours watching shows we don't enjoy, keeping up with plotlines we don't care about, just so we can nod along when someone else mentions them.

But the Parasite is lying. And the proof is in the question of what actually makes someone relevant.

Think about the people you most enjoy talking to. The ones whose company you genuinely seek out. The ones who make you feel seen, heard, understood.

Are they enjoyable because they've seen every show? Are they interesting because they've consumed every piece of cultural content currently trending? Or are they interesting because they're curious, present, and genuinely engaged when they talk to you?

The best conversations rarely hinge on having consumed the same content. They hinge on attention, on curiosity, on the quality of presence someone brings to the exchange. You've had deep, memorable conversations about things neither of you had seen, and shallow, forgettable conversations about shows you'd both watched. The content was never the point. The connection was.

What makes someone interesting to talk to — the list of shows they've watched, or the quality of attention they bring to the conversation?

The answer is obvious. And it reveals something important: Cultural relevance isn't built by watching everything. It's built by presence, curiosity, and genuine engagement.

But here's the twist. The queue erodes exactly those qualities.

The person who spends every evening watching is not more present—they're less present. They're not more curious—they're numbed out, foggy, overstimulated. They're not more engaged in their conversations—they're distracted, half-listening, always vaguely thinking about what to watch next.

The queue doesn't make you relevant. It makes you absent. It takes the very qualities that would make you interesting to talk to and buries them under hours of passive consumption.

You weren't becoming more culturally connected by watching everything. You were becoming less connected to the people actually in front of you.

Now let's look at the final piece of this illusion. The fear that if someone brings up a show you haven't seen, you'll look stupid. You'll be caught out. You'll have nothing to say.

You know the familiar pressure. Someone mentions a popular show, and you feel the urge to nod along, to pretend familiarity, to avoid the awkward admission that you haven't seen it. I need to have watched it, you think. Otherwise I'll look out of touch.

But what would actually happen if you simply said, "I haven't seen it — what do you like about it?"

Think about that for a moment. Not the imagined version. The real version. Someone mentions a show they're excited about. You say you haven't seen it. You ask them what they like about it. What happens next?

The conversation opens. The other person gets to talk about something they're passionate about. You get to learn something genuine about them. A real exchange begins — not a transaction of cultural references, but an actual connection.

The person who asks a real question creates connection. The person who pretends to have seen everything just nods.

Not having seen something is not a social liability. It's an opportunity. It's a chance to be curious, to ask questions, to let the other person share something they care about. That's what real cultural participation looks like — not consuming everything, but engaging genuinely with the people around you.

So here's the reframe:

The fear of being out of the loop is the Parasite's social variant — manufactured dread, not a real need. The conversations you fear missing are mostly shallow and brief. And cultural relevance doesn't come from watching everything. It comes from being present, curious, and genuinely engaged. The queue doesn't make you relevant. It makes you absent.

The next time you feel that pang of anxiety about a show everyone's watching, pause. Ask yourself: Do I actually want to watch this? Or am I just afraid of being left out?

If it's the former — if the story genuinely pulls you, if you're curious, if you want to experience it — then by all means, watch it. Deliberately. Fully. As a choice.

But if it's the latter — if it's just the Parasite whispering its social fear — then notice that. Notice the fear for what it is: a manufactured dread, not a real need. And let it pass.

You don't need to watch everything to belong. You just need to be present where you actually are.

But there's another social illusion that hits even closer to home. It's the one that shows up in living rooms and bedrooms, on couches and in front of screens, night after night.

This is quality time. We're watching together. That counts.
